\emph{Overview}
In November 2026 the question banks of the computer exam in the Python
part of DD1317 had to be read, checked and rewritten in the two weeks
before a sitting.  The 21 banks held 218 questions written by others,
shared with another course, and not readable through Canvas's interface
for programs.  We did the work in one long session with a team of about
20 AI agents, the author deciding and the agents preparing the decisions.
This report opens with the exam and why its banks mattered, lets you find
the flaws in four invented items of the kinds we found, and then walks
through the workflow, the results and the lessons, ending with what you
would prepare, decide and keep for yourself if you did the same in your
own course.  No real exam question appears in it: every example is
invented and has the same flaw as a real one.

\emph{Learning objectives}
After this report you should be able to:
\begin{restatable}{lo}{RapportLOStakes}\label{RapportLOStakes}%
  explain why a single defective item weighs heavily in an exam that
  draws one question per position and grades each learning objective
  separately;
\end{restatable}
\begin{restatable}{lo}{RapportLOFlaws}\label{RapportLOFlaws}%
  recognize the common defects of programming exam items --- keys that
  fail when run, ordering items with several valid orders, cues to the
  key, options that refer to other options --- and say which check
  exposes each;
\end{restatable}
\begin{restatable}{lo}{RapportLOWorkflow}\label{RapportLOWorkflow}%
  describe a workflow in which agents prepare and verify and the teacher
  decides, and what each of its steps guards against;
\end{restatable}
\begin{restatable}{lo}{RapportLOAdapt}\label{RapportLOAdapt}%
  plan such a rewrite for a course of your own: what to prepare, what to
  decide, and what never to delegate.
\end{restatable}

\emph{Prerequisites}
You should have written or reviewed multiple-choice items for an exam.
No experience of AI agents is needed; the report says what they did, not
how to drive them.
