\section{Research Context}
Research question: Is the notation of named, prose-surrounded program
parts an established practice -- literate programming -- and does it
originate with Donald Knuth (Literate Programming, The Computer Journal
1984)?

A paper is RELEVANT if it describes, applies, evaluates, teaches or
criticises literate programming or its tools (WEB, CWEB, noweb, nuweb,
Sweave, knitr, R Markdown, Jupyter and other reproducible-document
tooling), or bears on the origin of the idea and its notation.

Assign exactly one of these four category tags:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  supports-claim: bears on the claim AND supports that literate
  programming is an established, living practice. Its original
  statement, tools that implement it, documented use in teaching, data
  analysis or reproducible research, and descendants of the idea.
\item
  qualifies-claim: bears on the claim BUT contradicts or narrows it.
  Evidence that literate programming was never widely adopted in
  ordinary programming, that programmers find it impractical or
  academic, that its tools impose costs, or any other criticism of the
  practice or of the claim that the notation is established.
\item
  adjacent-subtopic: computer science or software engineering work with
  NO bearing on whether literate programming is established or where it
  came from. Documentation generation in general, program comprehension
  studies not about literate programming, notebook tooling studied only
  for other properties, code-summarisation or large-language-model code
  generation that merely mentions the term.
\item
  off-topic-false-hit: not about literate programming at all. Literacy
  research, language education, reading and writing instruction, or any
  other unrelated use of the words ``literate'', ``literacy'' or
  ``programming'' (e.g.~linear programming, dynamic programming, TV
  programming).
\end{itemize}
